⭐ 2.7.2 Explain what you calculated and how it connects to the interpretation of $R^2$. (What’s the total sum of squared errors? Why do we need to use it?)

🍓: I calculated the coefficient of determination, R^2, as 1 minus the ratio of the residual sum of squares (SSres) to the total sum of squares (SStot). SSres measures the squared deviation between each predicted value and its actual value, summed across all observations, the values are squared so that positive and negative residuals don't cancel each other out when summed. SStot measures the squared deviation between each actual y-value and the overall mean of y, representing the total variability in the data if you ignored the model entirely and just predicted the mean brain weight every time.

SStot matters because SSres alone isn't interpretable on its own, its magnitude depends on the scale of the data. SStot gives a baseline to compare against / the error of the simplest possible model with no predictors. R^2 expresses SSres relative to that baseline, which is what makes it comparable across models and datasets. A value close to 1 means SSres is small relative to SStot and a value close to 0 means the model does no better than that baseline.

🍓: Yes, it matters which space you calculate R^2 in. Re-calculating R^2 in the untransformed (raw) space, using the log model's back-transformed predictions (ŷ_raw = exp(ŷ_log)) compared against the actual raw brain weights, gives: SSres = 4,607,771.92, SStot = 52,790,553.53, and R^2 = 0.9127.

This does not match the R^2 = 0.9208 calculated in log space in 2.7.1, even though both numbers describe the same underlying model. The discrepancy exists because R^2 is not invariant under nonlinear transformations of the response variable. A small, roughly constant residual in log space becomes a multiplicative error in raw space, so the same log-space error translates into a tiny absolute deviation for a small-bodied animal but a very large absolute deviation for the largest animals in the dataset. Since SSres in raw space is dominated by squared absolute errors, those large-animal residuals disproportionately inflate SSres once back-transformed, pulling R^2 down relative to its log-space value.

SStot itself doesn't change between spaces in a way that's model-dependent because it only depends on the raw y-values and their mean.

3.3 Understanding Regularizer Penalty¶

❓ What would happen to the loss as $\lambda$ changes? What does this mean for the weights and overall loss?

✅Answer: As λ increases, the penalty on large weights grows, so the model shrinks the weights to reduce that penalty (with lasso, some weights can go to zero); as λ decreases toward 0, the weights drift back toward the unregularized OLS solution. Because OLS weights are the minimizer of SSE alone, moving away from them to satisfy a penalty can only increase training SSE. Smaller λ gets closer to the OLS fit but risks overfitting, so λ is trading off training fit against generalization.

3.4 Understanding Ridge and Lasso¶

❓ Is the loss for the L1 or L2 regularizer greater? In general, how does L1 vs L2 regularization affect the weights?

✅Answer: Here, L2 (ridge) loss is greater. In general, L1 can zero weights out (sparse, does feature selection) and L2 shrinks weights toward zero but keeps all of them nonzero (dense, but smaller). If |w| > 1, squaring makes it bigger and leads to L2 penalty > L1 penalty. If |w| < 1, squaring makes it smaller and leads to L2 penalty < L1 penalty.
